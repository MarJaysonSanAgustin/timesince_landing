Streak counters are everywhere. Duolingo, Snapchat, GitHub's contribution graph, half the fitness apps on your phone. They all reduce a habit to one number: how many days in a row.

It works, for a while. A growing streak feels good. Then one day you're sick, or traveling, or just forget, and the number resets to zero. Not "a little lower." Zero. All the progress you built is gone, and the thing you were doing to feel *better* about your habits now feels like a scoreboard you lost.

## The problem isn't you, it's the model

Streaks assume two things that aren't true for most of what you actually want to keep up with:

1. **That the habit happens every single day.** Some do: brushing your teeth, taking a daily medication. But most things worth tracking don't run on a 24-hour clock. Watering a succulent, calling your parents, replacing an HVAC filter, stretching before a run: these have their own natural rhythm, often measured in days or weeks, not "daily or bust."
2. **That a miss means failure.** Behavioral researchers have a name for what happens after a streak breaks: people don't just lose one day, they often abandon the habit entirely. Once the perfect record is gone, there's nothing left to protect, so the whole thing unravels. The mechanism meant to build consistency ends up being the reason it collapses.

Put those two things together and you get a tool that punishes the exact behavior (doing something every few days instead of every day) that it should be designed to support.

<Callout>
**A quick gut check:** if a habit's natural cadence is "every 4-5 days" but you're tracking it with a daily streak, the tool is fighting your actual schedule. That's a sign to switch what you're measuring, not to try harder.
</Callout>

## Track "time since," not "days in a row"

The fix is a small reframe with a big effect: instead of asking *"did I do this today?"*, ask *"how long has it been since I did this?"*

That single change removes the all-or-nothing trap. There's no streak to protect, so there's nothing to lose by missing a day. If you water your plants every 5 days and it's been 4, that's normal, not a near-miss. If it's been 12, that's useful information, not a guilt trip. The number just tells you the truth about your actual rhythm, and you decide what to do with it.

This is also a better match for how memory actually works. "When did I last do this?" is a question you can answer honestly by checking a number. "Did I keep my streak alive?" is a question that only has one acceptable answer, which is exactly why it's so stressful to face after a bad week.

## What this looks like in practice

A few habits worth tracking by elapsed time instead of a streak:

- **Irregular chores**: replacing water filters, rotating tires, cleaning gutters
- **Relationship maintenance**: calling a parent, checking in with an old friend
- **Quitting something**: smoking, vaping, a habit you're trying to break, where the goal is simply "as long as possible," and a slip should restart the count without erasing the fact that you'd made it that far
- **Self-care that doesn't fit a daily box**: therapy sessions, skincare routines, stretching

This is the whole premise behind TimeSince: no streaks, no pressure, no guilt. Your counter climbs while you're going, and simply restarts if you slip, with no shame, no broken-streak penalties. It's a small shift in framing, but it changes how it feels to miss a day: from a failure to erase, to a number that just keeps telling you the truth.
